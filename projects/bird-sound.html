Bird Sound Synthesizer ASM

Build it in two stages. First make each button play a simple tone on the buzzer. Then pick one of the four birds and play its call properly: pitch from a timer interrupt, note lengths from a second timer, and the call itself stored as data in program memory.

The two stages

The project is built in two stages. Stage 1 gets sound out of the buzzer with the simplest method there is, so that you know your buttons, your buzzer and your pin assignments all work. Stage 2 replaces that method with a timer interrupt and plays one of the four bird calls from a table.

Stage 1 requirements Checkpoint

Make the buzzer sound under button control, using only what you already know from the I/O examples. Do not use interrupts in this stage, and you do not need a table. The point is a clean, controllable tone and correct pin assignments.

#Requirement
S1Each of the four buttons K1–K4 plays a different tone on the buzzer. The four tones must be clearly distinguishable by ear.
S2A tone sounds while its button is held down, and stops when the button is released.
S3The matching LED (D1–D4) is on while its tone plays, and off otherwise.
S4The pitch is produced by toggling the buzzer pin in a delay loop. No interrupts in this stage.
S5When no button is pressed, the buzzer is silent and its pin is left HIGH.
S6You state the four frequencies you aimed for and measure what the buzzer actually produced, with a tuner or spectrogram app on your phone.

Deliverables. Your .asm source, a live demo of S1–S5, and a note of at most one page giving: the delay calculation for one of your tones, the four target frequencies, the four measured frequencies, and the difference between them.

Pin assignments are on the pin map: buzzer on P2.5, K1–K4 on P3.1, P3.0, P3.2, P3.3, and D1–D4 on P2.0–P2.3. Remove the LCD module during this project, because it shares P2.5. LED D6 is also on P2.5, so it glows faintly while a tone plays. That is normal.

Stage 2 requirements Final

Choose one of the four bird calls and make your board play it. The delay loop from stage 1 is replaced by a timer interrupt, and the call comes from a data table instead of from code. The demo and grading are based on this list.

#Requirement
R1Pressing the button that belongs to your chosen call plays that call once, to the specification in The four bird calls.
R2The matching LED (D1–D4) is on while the call plays and off otherwise.
R3Holding the button does not repeat the call. It must be released and pressed again.
R4The tone is a square wave on the buzzer pin generated by the Timer 0 interrupt. No delay loops for pitch in this stage.
R5Note durations are timed with Timer 1 using a 5 ms time base (polled or interrupt-driven).
R6The call is stored as a data table in program memory and played by a routine that reads that table, not as a block of code per note.
R7When no call is playing, the buzzer is silent and its pin is left HIGH.
R8Measured pitch is within ±3% of the specification. Check it with a tuner or spectrogram app on your phone.

Deliverables. Your commented .asm source and a live demo of R1–R8. There is no written report: instead, every member of the group answers oral questions about the code during the demo. Bring your stage 1 program as well, because you may be asked to compare the two.

Your other three buttons may keep their stage 1 tones or do nothing. Adding more bird calls is bonus work.

The four bird calls

Each call is a pattern of frequency sweeps and rests, simplified to what a buzzer and a 5 ms time base can play. Pick one of the four for stage 2, and use the button listed for it. Use Listen to hear the target: a square wave at the specified frequencies, like your buzzer.

How a sweep is defined

A sweep from f1 to f2 lasting T in steps of s has n = T / s notes, each held for s. Note i (0 … n−1) has frequency

fi = f1 + (f2 − f1) × c(u),   u = i / (n − 1)
Curvec(u)Shape
linearuConstant rate of change
fast1 − (1 − u)²Changes quickly at first, then levels off, like a whistled slur
slowu²Starts slowly and changes quickly at the end

The first note is exactly f1 and the last exactly f2. The tables have many notes, so it is sensible to calculate them with a spreadsheet or a short script. Include it with your submission.

Background

Making a pitch with a passive buzzer

A passive buzzer only sounds while its drive signal keeps changing. Toggling the pin every half period produces a square wave, so a tone of frequency f needs a toggle every T/2 = 1/(2f) seconds. On this board the buzzer is driven through a PNP transistor.

Stage 1: a tone without interrupts

Without interrupts, your own code has to toggle the pin, so the pitch depends on how long the program takes between toggles. One machine cycle is 12 crystal periods, about 1.085 µs on this board. Work out your own timing, then measure what the buzzer actually produces.

Timer 0 in mode 1

With the 11.0592 MHz crystal in 12T mode, a timer counts 921 600 times per second. In mode 1 it counts up from the value you load into TH0:TL0 and sets TF0 (and requests an interrupt) when it rolls over from FFFFH to 0000H. To overflow after N counts, load:

reload = 65536 − N

Mode 1 does not reload itself, so your ISR must reload the timer on every overflow. Responding to the interrupt and reloading takes time of its own, which is added to every half period, so the pitch you measure will not be quite the pitch you calculated. Measuring that difference and correcting for it is part of meeting R8.

More detail: Wiki: Timers · Wiki: Interrupts · Wiki: Clock & timing.

A 5 ms time base with Timer 1

Every note in the specification is a whole number of 5 ms steps, so R5 asks for a 5 ms time base from Timer 1. Work out the reload value yourself and verify it (see Milestone 1).

Storing sounds as tables

R6 asks you to describe the call as data rather than as code. How you encode a note, a rest and the end of the call is your design decision: invent a format, document it in your source, and make one routine play anything written in it. Constants are placed in program memory with DB and read back with MOVC A,@A+DPTR, as in the 7-segment example.

Design notes

How you structure the program is entirely up to you. These are questions worth answering before you write much code, not a required design:

  • What has to happen on every single interrupt, and what can wait for the main program?
  • The main program chooses the pitch and the interrupt routine uses it. What could go wrong if the interrupt arrives halfway through a change?
  • What state are the timer, its flag and the buzzer pin left in when a note ends, a rest starts, or the call finishes (R7)?
  • How will you tell a new press from a button that is simply still held down (R3)?

Register banks, the stack and program memory are covered in the 8051 Wiki. The starter template gives the pin names and vector addresses; everything else in it is yours to change or delete.

Milestones

Show each milestone to the lab instructor as you reach it. Milestone 0 is the stage 1 checkpoint; the rest belong to stage 2.

  1. Buttons and buzzer (stage 1). Each of K1–K4 plays its own tone while held, with the matching LED on, meeting S1–S6.
  2. Time base. Using only your Timer 1 routine, blink LED D8 (P2.7) at 1 Hz (500 ms on, 500 ms off). Ten blinks must take 10 s ± 0.2 s by stopwatch.
  3. Interrupt tone. While its button is held, the buzzer plays a steady 3000 Hz tone generated by the Timer 0 interrupt. It is silent otherwise.
  4. Your bird call. The button plays your chosen call from a table, with its LED lit, meeting R1–R8.

Bonus (up to +10)

  • More birds: a second and third call on their own buttons, to the same specification (+5 each).
  • Dawn chorus: pressing two buttons together plays every call you implemented, one after another.
  • Your own bird: design a fifth call of your own and play it from a key on the 4×4 keypad. Include its specification.
  • Spectrogram: include a phone spectrogram (for example Spectroid on Android) of your call next to the plot on this page, and comment on the differences.

Oral questions

There is no written report. Instead, 40 of the 100 marks come from the oral questions: at the demo, each member of the group is questioned individually about the program you submitted. Marks are given for your own answers, so every member must understand the whole program, not only the part they wrote.

You may keep your source in front of you. Expect questions of these kinds:

  • Your numbers. Where does a particular reload value come from? Recalculate it, at the desk, for a frequency the examiner names.
  • Your table. Explain your encoding, then read a few bytes of your own table aloud and say what they play.
  • Your interrupt. Walk through what happens between the timer overflowing and the next instruction of the main program. Why is each instruction in the routine there?
  • Your measurements. What pitch did you measure, how far was it from the specification, and what did you change?
  • Change it now. Which line would you edit to make the call play an octave lower, or twice as fast? Do it, reassemble and show us.
  • Break it. What would happen if this instruction were deleted, or if the interrupt were disabled here?
  • Stage 1 against stage 2. What can the processor do now that it could not do while the delay loop was running?

You are responsible for every line you submit, including anything adapted from an example or written by another member of your group. "My teammate wrote that part" does not earn marks.
