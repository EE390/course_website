Reading a Spectrogram

Support material for the Bird Sound Synthesizer project: how to turn a recording of a real bird into the frequency numbers that go in your table.

What a spectrogram shows

A spectrogram is a picture of sound over time. Time runs left to right, frequency runs bottom to top, and brightness shows how much energy the recording has at that frequency at that instant. A bird call shows up as one or more bright bands: a band that climbs is a rising sweep, a band that falls is a falling sweep, a flat band is a steady tone, and a gap with nothing bright in it is a rest.

Tools that show you one

  • Audacity (free, desktop, Windows/Mac/Linux) — the most accurate option for a saved recording. Open the audio file, then switch its track's view to Spectrogram from the track's dropdown menu. You can zoom in on a single syllable and read frequencies straight off the vertical axis. Use this for the recording you found on eBird.
  • Spectroid (free, Android) — turns your phone's microphone into a live spectrogram. Point it at your own buzzer to check what your board is actually producing, for R8 and for the comparison spectrogram in R9. On iOS, search the App Store for a free "spectrogram" or "spectrum analyzer" app that shows a scrolling frequency-vs-time view — several exist; any one that lets you read off a frequency axis will do.

Use Audacity on the source recording (accurate, works from the file itself) and a phone app on your own buzzer (you need to measure a live sound, not a file). Mixing the two — for example recording your buzzer through a laptop mic into Audacity — works too if that's easier for your group.

Worked example: measuring one syllable

This is the same syllable used in the worked example on the main project page, as if it had been traced from a real recording. Zoomed into one rising syllable in Audacity's spectrogram view, the bright band starts at about 3000 Hz, ends at about 4400 Hz, and the whole syllable lasts about 30 ms. Read off a few points along the band rather than just the endpoints — if they fall roughly on the fast curve from How a sweep is defined rather than a straight line, use that curve when you build the table.

Do this for each syllable of your call, measure the gaps between them the same way, and you have everything the sweep/rest table needs: a list of (f1, f2, duration, curve) for each syllable and a duration for each rest.
